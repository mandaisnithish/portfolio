Case study · AGS Roadways

Late deliveries weren't a route problem. They were a scheduling problem.

Late rate by departure hour 07:00–09:00 = peak failure window
The problem, as it was being solved

AGS attributed late deliveries to route complexity and vehicle availability, and optimised accordingly. Dispatchers estimated ETAs from accumulated experience with no formal method, so there was no way to tell whether delays were structural or random, or which part of the operation was producing them.

Approach, and why

EDA came before modelling, specifically to find out what the data actually tracked versus what people assumed it tracked. Gradient boosting over simple regression, because trip characteristics interact non-linearly. A heavy load matters more on some routes, in some weather. The configuration was chosen for interpretability on purpose: the goal wasn't accuracy alone, it was being able to explain an output to a dispatcher who had to trust it.

What it found

Departure hour explained late deliveries better than route distance, which pointed at congestion rather than route complexity. A fuel type × vehicle load interaction showed diesel on heavy loads running 23% more expensive per trip. Three routes accounted for 61% of SLA failures, not because they were hard but because they were consistently scheduled into the peak congestion window.

The trade-off

Recall over raw accuracy, because a missed late-delivery pattern costs more than a false alarm. The decision threshold moved from the default 0.5 down to 0.42, accepting more false positives to catch more real operational failures.

What changed

Management shifted attention from route optimisation to departure scheduling. The fuel finding became a procurement question. Forecast error fell 15%, operating costs 8%, and leadership stopped making Monday decisions against a spreadsheet built the previous Thursday.

Current work

An instrument you drive from a browser

What it is

Control software for laboratory test equipment: a single-page web interface over a Python backend, running on an embedded Linux board so the instrument is usable on its own without a laptop attached.

The architectural decision

The first version talked to the hardware directly from the browser. That put the transport in the wrong place. One tab held the port, so one tab held the instrument, and nothing worked across a network. Moving that layer behind the backend meant the browser stopped being a driver and became a client, which is what made multiple users and network access possible at all.

What it taught me

Shared hardware needs an ownership model, not just an interface. Most of the hard problems were concurrency: who holds a resource, what happens when a reading fails mid-run, how a second user is told no. I wrote an architecture reference and reviewed my own code against it, because those failure modes only surface once you have to describe the system to someone else.

When to trust a forecast

Prediction vs actual Shaded = uncertainty band
The question

Can a model learn anything meaningful in a market defined by noise, and if it can, when should you believe it?

Approach

An LSTM, because price at any moment depends on recent history in a way plain regression can't hold. Rolling momentum and volatility features were added less to improve the headline number than to give the model a sense of market regime, which changed how it behaved under high volatility.

What it found

Prediction intervals widened sharply during regime shifts, which is the correct behaviour. A model that admits uncertainty honestly is more useful than one that projects false confidence, and that's the finding I'd actually defend.

Imbalanced classification

The cost of being wrong

ROC · fraud classifier Marker = threshold 0.42
The real problem

A model optimised for accuracy on an imbalanced dataset predicts "not fraud" almost every time and still scores 99%. That isn't a detector, it's a rubber stamp.

Approach

SMOTE for the 97:3 class imbalance, not because it’s standard but because without it the model never learns what fraud looks like. XGBoost because the signal lives in interactions between transaction size, time of day and merchant category that logistic regression flattens out.

The trade-off, stated

Threshold dropped from 0.5 to 0.42, deliberately accepting more false positives to cut false negatives. Whether that's the right call depends entirely on the cost ratio in the specific business, which is the part worth arguing about. SHAP made each flag explainable to the reviewer who had to action it.

Risk scoring

A probability isn't a reason

Precision–recall Gold = ensemble · slate = logistic baseline
Why interpretability was the requirement

In credit decisions a score alone isn't actionable. An officer needs to know why a customer is high risk, because a decline without a reason isn't defensible to the customer or the regulator.

Approach

An XGBoost and logistic regression ensemble: the first for predictive strength, the second for the calibration that makes a 0.7 mean roughly 70%. SHAP was built into the output so every prediction arrived with its top three drivers in language a reviewer could read.

What it found

Churn and default shared leading indicators: customers showing early churn signals were around three times more likely to default within 90 days. That cross-signal only appeared once both models existed side by side.

How I think

The reasoning behind the work.

The process matters as much as the output.

On ambiguous problems

I start by asking what decision the analysis is meant to support, before touching the data. If I can't say what someone would do differently based on the output, the problem isn't scoped yet.

At AGS, "why are deliveries late?" turned out to be two questions: a scheduling one and a procurement one. Answering both meant separating them rather than treating it as one modelling task.

On model choice

The simplest model that answers the question, not the most impressive one. If a logistic regression gives a decision-maker enough confidence to act, XGBoost is complexity nobody asked for.

I move to something heavier when the problem genuinely calls for it, for non-linear interactions or temporal dependence, and when I can explain why to the person who'll use it.

On evaluating success

A good validation metric is necessary and not sufficient. Does it reflect what the decision-maker cares about? Optimising accuracy when the real concern is recall is a common and expensive mistake.

The harder question: did anyone act differently? If the output sits in a report that changes no behaviour, the numbers didn't save it.

On communicating insight

Lead with the implication, not the finding. "Departure hour has an importance of 0.67" tells a logistics director nothing. "You're losing more time to scheduling than to route length" tells them where to look.

And make the uncertainty explicit. A recommendation with honest bounds is worth more than a confident one that hides how much the model doesn't know.

Trade-off thinking

Every model choice is a trade-off

Accuracy against interpretability: the decision that shapes applied work most and appears in portfolios least.

Why not always reach for XGBoost?

A model at 94% that nobody trusts is worth less than one at 87% a logistics director will actually act on. Interpretability is a feature, not a concession.

When complexity earns its place

An LSTM for price sequences: linear models fundamentally can't hold temporal memory. The complexity is justified by the structure of the problem, not by wanting to look clever.

The fraud detection middle ground

XGBoost because the signal lives in interactions linear models miss, with SHAP layered on so every flag stays explainable to the human reviewing it.
