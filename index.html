01 · Featured Case Study
Late Deliveries Weren't a Route Problem They Were a Scheduling Problem
Late Deliveries by Departure Hour · n=10,247 trips
07:00–09:00 AM = peak failure window
Loading chart...
The Problem Being Solved Incorrectly AGS Roadways was attributing late deliveries to route complexity and vehicle availability and optimising accordingly. Before this project, dispatchers estimated ETAs using accumulated experience with no formal method. There was no way to tell whether delays were structural or random, or which part of the operation was actually causing them.
Approach & Why Started with EDA before modelling specifically to understand what the data was actually tracking vs what people assumed it tracked. Used gradient boosting over simpler regression because trip characteristics interact non linearly (a heavy load matters more on certain routes, in certain weather). Chose an interpretable model configuration deliberately: the goal wasn't just accuracy it was being able to explain the output to a dispatcher who needed to trust it.
Key Insights Departure hour explained late deliveries more than route distance meaning congestion, not route complexity, was the primary driver. A fuel_type × vehicle_load interaction showed diesel on heavy loads cost 23% more per trip than petrol. Three routes (R-14, R-22, R-38) accounted for 61% of all SLA failures not because they were inherently difficult, but because they were consistently scheduled during peak congestion windows.
Trade-off Made Prioritised recall over raw accuracy: a missed anomaly (undetected late delivery pattern) costs more than a false alarm. Set the decision threshold at 0.42 rather than the default 0.5 accepting more false positives to catch more real operational failures.
What Changed Management shifted attention from route optimisation to departure scheduling. The fuel finding became a procurement consideration. MAE reduced 15% vs baseline. Operational costs fell 8%. Leadership stopped receiving a five hour old Excel sheet and started making decisions against live dashboards.

When to Trust a Forecast Bitcoin Sequence Modelling
LSTM vs Actual · BTC Price Sequence
Dashed = prediction · Shaded = uncertainty band
Loading chart...
The core question Can a model learn meaningful patterns in a market defined by noise and if so, when should you trust its output?

Used LSTM because the price at any moment depends on recent history in a way standard regression can't capture. Rolling statistical features (momentum, volatility windows) were added not to improve accuracy numerically, but to give the model context about market regime a deliberate choice that changed how the predictions behaved under high volatility conditions.

Key finding: Prediction intervals widened substantially during regime shifts which is actually the correct behaviour. A model that expresses uncertainty honestly is more useful than one that projects false confidence.

The Cost of Being Wrong Fraud Detection with Calibrated Thresholds
ROC Curve · AUC = 0.964
Gold dot = threshold 0.42 (recall-optimised)
Loading chart...
The real problem A model optimised for accuracy on an imbalanced dataset will predict "not fraud" almost every time and still score 99%. That's not a fraud detector it's a rubber stamp.

Applied SMOTE to address the 97:3 class imbalance not because it's the standard approach, but because without it the model would never learn what fraud actually looks like. Used XGBoost because ensemble methods handle the feature interactions (transaction size, time of day, merchant category) better than logistic regression in this domain.

Trade off made explicit: Lowered the decision threshold from 0.5 to 0.42 deliberately accepting more false positives (flagging legitimate transactions) to reduce false negatives (missing actual fraud). Whether that trade-off is right depends on the cost ratio in the specific business context. SHAP attribution made each flag explainable to a reviewer.

Probability Isn't Enough — Credit Risk Scoring With Explainable Outputs

Why interpretability mattered here In credit and churn decisions, a model score alone isn't actionable a credit officer needs to know why a customer is high risk, not just that they are. A "deny" decision without a reason isn't defensible.

Built an XGBoost + logistic regression ensemble: XGBoost for predictive accuracy, logistic regression for the probability calibration that makes output scores meaningful (so a 0.7 really means roughly 70% risk). SHAP attribution was built into the output so each prediction came with the top three factors driving it in language a credit reviewer could interpret.

Key insight: Churn and default risk shared overlapping leading indicators customers showing early churn signals were 3× more likely to default within 90 days. That cross signal wasn't visible until both models were built and compared.

Trade-off Thinking

Every model choice is a trade-off

Accuracy vs interpretability the most important decision in applied data science that rarely appears in portfolios.

Why not always use XGBoost?

A model with 94% accuracy that nobody trusts is less useful than one with 87% accuracy that a logistics director will actually act on. Interpretability is a feature, not a limitation.

When complexity earns its place

LSTM for Bitcoin price sequences: linear models fundamentally cannot capture temporal memory. The complexity is justified by the structure of the problem not by a desire to impress.

The fraud detection sweet spot

XGBoost chosen because the fraud signal lives in interactions (time × amount × merchant type) that linear models miss but SHAP was added to make every flag explainable to a human reviewer.

How I Think

The reasoning behind the work.

The process matters as much as the output.

On Ambiguous Problems

I start by asking what decision the analysis is supposed to support before touching the data. A vague question produces a vague answer. If I can't articulate what someone would do differently based on my output, the problem isn't scoped correctly yet.

At AGS, "why are deliveries late?" was actually two separate questions: a scheduling question and a procurement question. Answering both required treating them separately, not as one modelling task.

On Model Choice

I choose the simplest model that answers the question not the most impressive one. If a logistic regression gives a decision maker enough confidence to act, XGBoost is unnecessary complexity.

I move to complex models when the problem genuinely requires it non linear interactions, temporal dependencies and when I can explain why to someone who will use the output.

On Evaluating Success

A good metric on a validation set is necessary but not sufficient. I consider: does the metric reflect what the decision maker actually cares about? Optimising for accuracy when the real concern is recall is a common and costly mistake.

The deeper question: did someone act differently because of this analysis? If the output sits in a report that no one changes behaviour from, the project didn't succeed regardless of the numbers.

On Communicating Insights

I lead with the implication, not the finding. "Departure hour has a feature importance of 0.67" tells a logistics director nothing. "You're losing more time to scheduling than to route length" tells them where to focus.

I also try to make uncertainty explicit a recommendation with clear confidence bounds is more useful than a confident sounding one that conceals how much the model doesn't know.
